How do South African political memes use visuals and language to resist and challenge dominant digital narratives?

01 — Design Brief

What am I designing?

This module takes the research from RP300 and turns it into a digital product that responds to the issues identified in it. It begins with blue-sky discussion, then moves into a proposal and a clear project plan before any design work starts.

My RP300 paper, Meme Culture and Digital Resistance in South Africa: Visualizing Political and Social Activism in the Digital Age, asked how South African political memes use visuals and language to resist and challenge dominant digital narratives. Caus. is the attempt to build something out of that question rather than only write about it.

The theory it's built on

Shifman
  • Memes are cultural artefacts, not just images
  • They spread through imitation, remix and participation
  • Humour becomes political commentary
Mirzoeff
  • Countervisuality — "the right to look"
  • Memes become counter-visual tools
  • Reclaiming the power to represent political events
Amanda du Preez
  • Writes about affect, visibility and postcolonial digital culture in South Africa
  • Explains why memes in SA hit so hard

02 — Approach

How did I approach the problem?

Eight weeks, worked through in sequence — but I let the research overturn the framing rather than treating the concept as settled. The survey in the middle of the project changed what the app was for.

03 — Research

What did I discover?

I ran a survey of 21 people, mostly aged 18–24, alongside an empathy map and an audit of two existing platforms. I wanted to know two things: whether people actually treat memes as political, and whether anything happens after they share one.

The first question answered itself quickly. 81% had shared or seen a meme about a political or social issue, roughly 45% share memes daily, and 81% thought memes were an effective way to spread awareness. Motivation split between entertaining or provoking thought (47.6%) and criticising or satirising something (33.3%). The second question is where it got interesting.

81% said memes are an effective way to spread awareness about social issues
52.4% did not believe memes could get politically disengaged people to take action
61.9% had never attended a protest they first heard about online

So the audience believed in the reach and doubted the outcome. What did move people was specific: 57.1% said they'd take real-world action if it was an issue they already cared about, 19% if the meme came from a source they trusted, and 14.3% if taking part felt safe and accessible. 55% said they would attend an in-person event that started as an online meme campaign — which sits oddly against the 61.9% who never had. The intent is there; something in between is missing.

Humour is the gateway — people engage with causes when it doesn't feel preachy. But the pains clustered tightly: activism feeling performative, information overload as causes compete for attention, uncertainty about whether a meme or the campaign behind it is credible, and no clear route from an online reaction to an offline one.

Competitor analysis

I looked at Global Citizen and Ground News. Global Citizen already blends activism with entertainment and rewards participation with points, but its navigation — Festival, For You, Discover, Goals & Journeys — is vague and overlapping, and points appear everywhere without explaining what they convert into.

Ground News was the more useful failure. It exposes media bias brilliantly by showing left, centre and right coverage side by side, which is a genuinely counter-visual move. But the bias meter is passive: it tells you the split without explaining how the narratives differ. It's a dashboard, not a conversation. News is observed, never co-created — the exact opposite of how meme culture works, where critique becomes shared authorship. That contrast defined what Caus. needed to be.

04 — Defining Problems

What problems did I identify?

Primary problem

Memes spread awareness, but they rarely lead to real action.

A meme can reach more people faster than a news article, and the audience knows it. What's missing is the step after the share — nothing in the platforms people already use connects a meme to the campaign, petition or event it's actually about.

Supporting problem 01 — Trust

People are uncertain whether a meme, or the campaign behind it, is credible. 19% said they'd only act on something from a trusted source, and "unsure what's real online" appeared across both the empathy map and the personas.

Supporting problem 02 — Fragmentation

Organising is scattered across multiple apps, and causes rotate fast — yesterday's issue is old news before anyone turns up to anything. There's no single place where a cause, its content and its events sit together.

05 — Ideation

What solutions did I explore?

I explored the features people actually asked for in the survey rather than the ones I found most interesting to design. The survey's feature question gave me a ranked list to work from, and I weighted each one against whether it closed the awareness-to-action gap or just made the app busier.

Awareness tools Trending feeds and educational content — good reach, but this is the part that already works
Community tools Forums and group chats — builds the conversation, but doesn't move it offline
Direction chosen Bind every meme to a real action

06 — Design Process

How did I turn ideas into an interface?

The structure came before the styling. If the core idea was that a meme should always lead somewhere, the architecture had to make that path short — which meant the meme detail page became the hinge of the whole product.

Information architecture

Onboarding leads into the home page, which branches into eight areas: home feed, meme details, remix, map, profile, forum, petition and protest. Petitions and protests both link back to the meme that surfaced them, so the loop closes in both directions. Sharing or completing an action earns points.

User flow

Feed → Story → Call to action → Taking action → Sharing

Four flows carry the product. A user searches the feed, sees a meme's variants and remixes, opens its detail page and finds the campaign attached to it. From the meme detail they reach the protest and join it. On the map, dropped pins open protest cards. On the remix page they download the original, edit it, and post — earning points for the share.

Wireframes

Simple, low-fidelity layouts that show the basic structure and flow of the interface, without detailed design elements — enough to test whether the path from meme to action held up before committing to visuals.

Visual System Choice

Bittersweet (#FF6B54) as primary against Space Cadet (#2F2E41), with Alice Blue (#ECF3F7) and Lavender (#CFDCEF) as accents. Poppins throughout. The palette had to carry humour without looking like a joke app — warm enough to feel social, dark enough that a petition doesn't look out of place next to it.

07 — Reflection

What did I learn?

The most useful thing that happened in this project was being contradicted by my own survey. I went in from a research paper about how memes resist dominant narratives, which had already convinced me memes were powerful. The survey agreed — and then said, by a majority, that memes wouldn't get disengaged people to act. I could have designed around that, or treated it as the brief. Taking it as the brief is what turned Caus. from a meme app into an app about the distance between reacting and doing.

Ground News taught me more than the direct competitors did. It's a well-made product solving a real problem, and its weakness is structural rather than visual: it positions the user as an observer. Noticing that is what made me put remixing at the centre of Caus. rather than treating it as a side feature.

What I'd change: I didn't run usability testing within the eight weeks, and that's the clearest gap in the project. The whole design rests on an untested assumption — that a link from a meme to a petition actually gets tapped. I'd also want to test whether the points system motivates people or does the opposite, since the same survey that showed 57.1% interested in gamification also showed a widespread suspicion of activism that feels performative. Rewarding shares could easily reinforce the exact behaviour the app was built to move past.

I'd also want a bigger sample. 21 responses, heavily weighted toward 18–24, is enough to find a tension but not enough to size it.
